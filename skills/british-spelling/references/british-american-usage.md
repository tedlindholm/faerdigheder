# British and American Usage

Use these preferences in project-owned prose and interface text intended to use British English. They complement the spelling rules; they are not blind search-and-replace rules.

Preserve external identifiers, quotations, official names, and terms that describe a non-British context. For example, a UK address has a `postcode`, while a US address has a `ZIP code`.

## Instructions and Interface Text

| Prefer in British English | Common American equivalent |
| --- | --- |
| fill in the form | fill out the form |
| tick the box | check the box |
| write to us | write us |
| full stop | period |

Use `dot`, not `full stop`, when naming the character in a URL, filename, identifier, or command.

## Common Phrasing

| Prefer in British English | Common American equivalent |
| --- | --- |
| at the weekend | on the weekend |
| Monday to Friday | Monday through Friday |
| Have you finished yet? | Did you finish yet? |
| I've just sent it. | I just sent it. |
| different from | different than |

These are preferences, not claims that the American forms are always incorrect or unfamiliar in Britain. Preserve a speaker's dialect in quotations and dialogue.

## Check the Meaning

Some familiar words describe different things in each dialect:

- In Britain, the `ground floor` is at street level and the `first floor` is above it. In the US, the `first floor` is normally at street level.
- British `pants` usually means underwear; American `pants` means `trousers`.
- British `chips` are American `French fries`; American `chips` are British `crisps`.
- Use `bank holiday` only for an official UK bank holiday. Use `public holiday` as the general term.
